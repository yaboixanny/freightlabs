How to Run Google Ads for Logistics Companies: Full Tutorial for 2027

Book a Strategy Call

Google Ads can put your logistics company in front of shippers who are looking for the services you offer. But a click is not a customer. If your ads reach job seekers, personal movers, or shippers outside your service area, your budget can disappear without creating a useful sales conversation.

This guide walks through how to plan, launch, and improve a Search campaign for a freight broker, 3PL, carrier, or freight forwarder. The goal is simple: reach the right buyers, make it easy to request a quote, and learn which inquiries turn into real opportunities.

How Does Google Ads Work for Logistics Companies?

Google Ads lets you pay to appear when people search for terms related to your services. You choose your goals, locations, keywords, ads, and budget. Google then determines whether your ad can enter an auction when someone searches.

For example, a warehouse near Chicago might advertise to businesses searching for nearby pallet storage. A freight forwarder might focus on ocean freight from a market it serves. In both cases, the search tells you something about what the buyer needs. Your ad and landing page should make it clear whether you can help.

You do not have to do this alone. Our Google Ads services for logistics companies cover campaign planning, landing pages, tracking, and ongoing improvements.

Step 1: Decide What a Good Lead Looks Like

Before you open Google Ads, talk to sales and operations. Which services do you want to grow? Where can you take on more freight or warehouse volume? What information does your team need before it can prepare a quote?

  • Services: Choose a specific offer, such as LTL brokerage, drayage, cold storage, or customs support.
  • Coverage: List the markets, facilities, lanes, and customer locations you can actually serve.
  • Customer fit: Define the industries, freight types, volumes, and account sizes that make sense for your business.
  • Sales process: Decide who will receive inquiries, how quickly they can reply, and how they will record the outcome.

Start where you have a clear service and enough capacity to help. If your buyers rarely search for your offer, or your team cannot follow up with inquiries, work on those gaps before increasing ad spend.

Step 2: Set a Budget You Can Measure

There is no single budget or cost per lead that works for every logistics company. Competition, location, service, season, and lead quality all affect the numbers. Start with what you can afford to test and agree on how long you will give the campaign to collect useful data.

Work backward from the value of a customer. If a customer produces $10,000 in gross profit over the period you use for planning, and one in five qualified leads becomes a customer, a $200 qualified lead costs about $1,000 in acquisition spend per customer. That example leaves room for other costs, but it is not a target for every business. Use your own margins, close rates, and sales cycle.

Set a daily budget in Google Ads and monitor actual spending. Keep agency fees, landing page work, and tracking costs separate from the amount paid to Google.

Step 3: Organize Campaigns Around Real Services

A good account structure makes it easier to see which services bring the right inquiries. Separate campaigns when you need different budgets, locations, or goals. Within each campaign, group closely related searches so your ads and landing pages answer the same need.

For a freight forwarder, a starting structure could look like this:

  • Ocean freight campaign: Ad groups for full-container shipping and less-than-container-load services.
  • Customs support campaign: Ad groups for the clearance services your team provides.
  • Air freight campaign: Ad groups for general air freight and time-sensitive shipments.

A single useful page can serve several closely related searches. Create a separate page when the service, location, or buyer need is different enough to deserve one. Avoid making dozens of thin lane pages just to match keywords.

Step 4: Choose Keywords With Buying Intent

Think about what a shipper would type when they need the service you sell. A search for “3PL warehouse near Chicago” says more about the need than a broad search for “logistics.” Start with a focused list and build from the terms that bring relevant inquiries.

  • Service and location: “3PL warehouse Chicago” or “drayage Long Beach.”
  • Service and freight type: “temperature-controlled freight carrier” or “hazmat freight broker.”
  • Mode and route: “air freight from Guangzhou to Chicago” or “ocean freight Asia to US.”
  • Specific need: “cross-docking services near Dallas” or “customs clearance support.”

Choose match types with care

Google offers broad, phrase, and exact match. Exact match gives you more control but can reach fewer searches. Phrase match reaches a wider set of related searches, and broad match can reach wider still. Even exact match can include searches with the same meaning or intent; it is not limited to the exact words you typed.

For a new account, phrase and exact match can be useful ways to start with a focused list. Broad match may be worth testing when your tracking, budget, and review process can support it. Check the search terms report and make decisions from your own results. Google explains the options in its keyword match type guide.

Step 5: Add Negative Keywords

Negative keywords help prevent ads from appearing for searches that do not fit your offer. They are especially useful when a logistics term can also describe a job, consumer move, or software product.

Review terms such as “jobs,” “salary,” “training,” and “personal moving” if those searches are outside your goal. A service provider might also exclude software-related searches if it does not sell software. Add exclusions for locations, modes, or services you do not cover when the search data supports them.

Do not copy a long negative list without checking it. A word that filters waste for one company could block a good customer for another. Negative match types work differently from positive keywords, so review Google’s negative keyword guidance and check the search terms report regularly.

Step 6: Write Ads That Explain the Fit

Your ad should help the right buyer recognize your service. Name what you do, where you do it, and what the buyer can do next. Replace generic claims with useful details you can support.

Example: a focused warehouse ad

  • Headline ideas: “Warehouse Space Near Chicago,” “Pallet Storage and Distribution,” and “Request a Warehouse Quote.”
  • Description idea: “Need storage and distribution near Chicago? Tell us about your freight and get in touch with our team.”

Use those examples only if they describe your business. If you mention certifications, operating hours, response times, capacity, or customer names, make sure they are accurate and approved. Avoid promising a guaranteed result unless you can support the claim and honor it.

Use relevant ad assets too. Sitelinks can point to service or location pages. Callouts can highlight real capabilities, and call assets can make it easier to contact your team when someone is available to answer.

Step 7: Build a Landing Page for the Search

Sending every visitor to a generic homepage makes the buyer do extra work. If someone searches for cross-docking near Dallas, send them to a page that explains your cross-docking service and the area you serve.

A useful landing page should include:

  • A clear headline: Name the service and location or market when relevant.
  • Service details: Explain what you handle, where you operate, and what a good-fit shipment or account looks like.
  • Proof: Show relevant licenses, facilities, equipment, experience, or customer examples you can verify.
  • A simple next step: Make the quote form or contact option easy to find on desktop and mobile.
  • A useful form: Ask for the details your team needs to follow up, such as contact information and a brief description of the shipment. Add fields only when they serve a purpose.

Check that the page loads quickly, reads well on a phone, and sends inquiries to the right people. Then test the form yourself. The most persuasive ad will not help if the next step is confusing or broken.

Step 8: Set Up Conversion Tracking Before Launch

A conversion is an action you want to measure, such as a quote request or a call. Set up and test the actions that matter to your business before ads go live. Check that a successful submission is counted once and that test entries do not get mistaken for real leads.

A form fill is a starting point, not proof that an inquiry is a good fit. Record what happens next in your CRM or sales process: Was the company a shipper? Did it need a service you provide? Did the conversation become an opportunity?

When your tools and permissions support it, you can send qualified lead or closed-sale information back to Google Ads. This can help you compare campaigns against outcomes beyond the first form fill. Google describes the setup and current requirements in its enhanced conversions for leads guide. Make sure your data collection and sharing follow the rules and consent choices that apply to your business.

Step 9: Choose a Bidding Strategy and Launch

Your bidding choice should fit your goal, budget, and tracking. A new campaign may have little information about which clicks become good leads. If you use automated bidding, make sure the conversion actions it uses are meaningful and working. If you choose a click-focused approach, watch lead quality and spending closely.

There is no universal number of leads or fixed number of days that tells every account when to change strategies. Review the quality and volume of your data, your sales cycle, and how consistently conversions are recorded before making a change. Avoid judging a long sales cycle by the first few days of clicks.

Before launch, check your service areas, location settings, ad copy, landing pages, negative keywords, forms, call handling, and budget. Ask someone outside the account to go through the path from search to inquiry.

Step 10: Review Search Terms and Lead Quality

After launch, look at the searches that triggered your ads. Are people looking for the services and locations you actually cover? Are they shippers, or are you paying for jobs and personal moves? Add or adjust negatives when you find a clear mismatch.

Review the sales side as well. A campaign with fewer inquiries may be more useful if more of them fit your services and turn into real sales conversations. Compare spend, inquiries, qualified leads, opportunities, and customers where your data allows. Ask your sales team what the numbers miss.

A simple review routine

  • Early in the campaign: Check tracking, search terms, spend, and lead routing often enough to catch problems quickly.
  • Each month: Review lead quality with sales, compare the services and locations you are promoting, and decide what to test next.
  • As sales progress: Connect opportunities and customers back to campaigns when possible. Keep the long sales cycle in mind before making major budget decisions.

Common Google Ads Mistakes in Logistics

  1. Advertising every service at once. Start with a focused offer and a budget that lets you learn.
  2. Sending all traffic to the homepage. Give each buyer a page that answers the need behind the search.
  3. Ignoring irrelevant searches. Review search terms and update negatives as you learn.
  4. Counting every inquiry as a good lead. Bring sales feedback into the review.
  5. Targeting the wrong location. Think about where your buyers are, as well as where your service operates. Review your campaign’s location options.
  6. Making changes without checking the impact. Review automated recommendations and settings before accepting changes to bids, keywords, or budgets.
  7. Leaving calls and forms unanswered. Agree on who follows up and how the team records outcomes.

Should Logistics Companies Use Google Ads and SEO?

They can work well together. Google Ads can put an offer in front of buyers while a campaign is funded. SEO helps build useful pages and organic search visibility over time. Neither channel guarantees leads, and both need clear services, good pages, and sales follow-up.

Start with the services your business wants to grow. Use what you learn from paid search to inform the questions and topics you cover on your site. Our freight SEO guide explains how to build the organic side of that plan.

Ready to Build Your Logistics Google Ads Plan?

Start with one clear service, the buyers you can help, and a page that makes it easy to ask for a quote. Set up tracking before you spend, then use search terms and sales feedback to improve the campaign. That gives you a practical way to learn what is working and where your budget can go further.
